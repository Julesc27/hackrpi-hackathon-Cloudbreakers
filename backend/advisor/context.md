Cloudbreakers
Full Game Design Document

Small choices. Higher tomorrows.

Cloudbreakers is a progression-based settlement and exploration game about building a sustainable town in the sky. The player begins with almost nothing: one small floating island, a few residents, a fragile shelter, and limited supplies. By gathering resources, completing missions, connecting nearby islands, and making difficult trade-offs, the player gradually turns an isolated cloud outpost into a thriving network of sky settlements.

The game should feel hopeful and handmade rather than industrial or dystopian. Its world is painted in deep night blues, warm sunrise golds, soft cloud whites, turquoise water, lavender crystal, and plant greens. The settlement is made from wood, canvas, stone, recycled materials, windmills, solar panels, water collectors, bridges, gardens, clinics, towers, and airships. Everything should communicate that the town is being built by people who are inventive, cooperative, and trying to live responsibly in a delicate environment.
1. The player fantasy
The player is the founder and steward of a new sky settlement. They are not playing as a military commander or a wealthy city planner. They are rebuilding a home in an environment where every bridge, crop, water tank, and power source matters.

The central fantasy is:

Start with one small island and create a living, sustainable town that can reach across the sky without destroying the world that supports it.

The player should feel four stages of progress:
1. Survival: Keep a tiny group of residents supplied with food, water, and shelter.
2. Connection: Build bridges and routes to nearby resource islands.
3. Community: Grow from an outpost into a recognizable town with homes, farms, a clinic, markets, and public spaces.
4. Stewardship: Build a sustainable sky network that produces more renewable energy, water, and food than it consumes.
The game is not about reaching the largest population as quickly as possible. Uncontrolled growth can create water shortages, energy failures, pollution, and unhappy residents. The best town is balanced, resilient, and prepared for change.
2. The world of Cloudbreakers
Long before the game begins, the lower world became increasingly difficult to inhabit.

Rising seas, unstable weather, and damaged ecosystems forced communities to build upward. Early engineers discovered that certain islands of buoyant stone and dense cloud matter could remain suspended above the storms. These became the first sky islands.

The sky was never empty. It held old settlements, abandoned observatories, wind farms, ancient water systems, broken airship docks, and islands that had been separated from one another for generations. Some communities learned to cooperate. Others isolated themselves, exhausted their resources, or disappeared into the storm belt.

The player begins in the remains of a small former outpost called New Haven. New Haven contains the foundations of a shelter, a broken bridge anchor, and a dormant navigation beacon. Its people have enough supplies to survive for a short time, but not enough to remain isolated.

The player’s long-term objective is to restore the beacon network and establish a sustainable town called a Skystead. A Skystead is more than a collection of buildings. It is a connected community that produces renewable energy, manages water carefully, grows food, protects its residents, and shares resources with neighboring settlements.
3. Tone and storytelling style
The tone is optimistic, warm, and lightly humorous. Storms and shortages are serious, but the game should not feel bleak. Residents celebrate small victories, invent practical solutions, decorate bridges, and tell stories about the islands they discover.

The world can contain danger without becoming grim. A failed bridge may isolate a market for a turn. A damaged water collector may force the player to ration supplies. A storm may destroy crops or temporarily close an airship route. These events create meaningful decisions, not punishment for its own sake.

The writing should use short, concrete dialogue. Residents should sound like people who are busy, capable, and a little eccentric.

Examples:
- “The west bridge is holding, but it has started making a noise that concerns everyone.”
- “The greenhouse has produced enough tomatoes for a festival. The residents have voted to call it a festival.”
- “The clouds are moving strangely today. The old navigation tower says this is normal. The old navigation tower is usually wrong.”
- “Rain Island is close enough to see and far enough to be expensive.”
4. Core game loop
Each play session follows a simple loop:
1. Review the town’s resources and current conditions.
2. Read a mission, event, or story problem.
3. Decide whether to gather, build, expand, trade, upgrade, or prepare.
4. Spend resources on an action.
5. Advance the turn.
6. Apply production, consumption, weather effects, and consequences.
7. Receive progress toward missions and rewards.
8. Decide what the next expansion or upgrade should be.
A turn represents a short period in the town’s development. The player does not need to wait in real time. The player chooses when to end the turn after making the decisions they want to make.

The game should always present several reasonable options. The goal is not to discover one hidden correct answer. The goal is to understand trade-offs and build a strategy.
5. The starting experience
The opening begins with a cinematic view of a tiny cloud island drifting through a blue sky.

A few residents stand beside a damaged shelter. In the distance, three islands are visible through the clouds: a green forest island, a rain-filled island with reservoirs, and a crystalline island glowing with stored energy.

The player receives the first story message:

“New Haven is small, but it is still here. The beacon is dark, the pantry is nearly empty, and the nearest islands are beyond the broken bridge. We have enough material for one useful beginning. Choose carefully.”

The tutorial teaches one system at a time:
- Build the first shelter.
- Gather wood from the home island.
- Build a bridge anchor.
- Choose the first island to connect.
- Complete the first mission.
- Earn XP and unlock the next building.
The tutorial should not overwhelm the player with every resource, building, and system. It should make the first bridge feel meaningful.
6. Resources and town statistics
The game uses a small group of readable resources. Every resource should have a visible purpose and a clear relationship to the town.
Wood
Wood is the first construction material. It is used for shelters, bridges, platforms, markets, and early upgrades. Forest Island increases wood production. Later, recycling and material research reduce the amount of new wood the town needs.
Water
Water is consumed by residents, farms, clinics, and some industrial buildings. Rain Island and water collectors provide the primary supply. Water shortages reduce happiness and can reduce population if they continue.
Food
Food is produced by gardens, farms, greenhouses, and certain islands. Population growth consumes food. A surplus increases happiness and makes the town more resilient during emergencies.
Energy
Energy powers water systems, clinics, airship docks, research buildings, and advanced construction. Solar farms, wind turbines, and Crystal Island provide renewable energy. A town with low energy may have to choose which services remain active.
Cloud coins
Cloud coins are a progression currency earned from missions, discoveries, trade, and special events. They can be used for cosmetic items, convenience upgrades, and certain town improvements. They should not replace the resource strategy of the game.
Population
Population measures the number of residents living in the town. More residents unlock opportunities and increase production, but they also increase food and water consumption.

Population should grow through housing, missions, and story events rather than being a number that rises without consequences.
Happiness
Happiness represents quality of life. It rises when residents have food, shelter, safety, public spaces, and successful festivals. It falls during shortages, long isolation, dangerous storms, and harsh rationing. Happiness affects mission outcomes and population growth.
Sustainability
Sustainability is the town’s long-term health score. It increases through renewable power, water conservation, recycling, food stability, low pollution, and balanced expansion. It decreases when the player relies on wasteful emergency solutions or expands without infrastructure.
7. Islands and expansion
The map is made of floating islands connected by bridges, platforms, towers, and later airship routes. The player starts with one island and gradually reveals more of the sky map.
New Haven: the home island
New Haven is the player’s starting island. It contains the first shelter site, a small gathering area, an inactive beacon, and enough basic resources for the opening mission. It becomes the administrative and cultural center of the final town.

Buildings include:
- First shelter.
- Town hall or beacon plaza.
- Central bridge anchor.
- Starter garden.
- Later, market, clinic, archive, and public square.
Verdant Reach: Forest Island
Verdant Reach is covered in trees, vines, herbs, and old wooden platforms. It provides wood, food, and medicinal plants. It is the easiest island to connect to and is ideal for players who want to expand construction quickly.

Story question: How much of the forest should the town harvest, and how much should it protect?
Rain Island
Rain Island contains natural reservoirs, suspended waterfalls, water towers, and old filtration equipment. It provides water and unlocks better water-management buildings.

Story question: Should the town redirect water immediately for population growth, or restore the island’s natural collection system first?
Prism Shelf: Crystal Island
Prism Shelf is rich in luminous crystals that store wind and sunlight. It provides energy and unlocks research, advanced bridges, and later navigation technology.

Story question: Can the crystals be used without destabilizing the island?
Mistral Heights: Wind Island
Mistral Heights contains old wind turbines and exposed ridges. It provides renewable energy and introduces wind patterns, weather forecasting, and airship routes.

Story question: Is the town willing to accept noisy machinery and altered wind paths in exchange for reliable energy?
The Glass Gardens
The Glass Gardens are a greenhouse island built by a vanished community. They contain seed libraries, rare crops, and automated growing beds. This island expands the food system and introduces crop diversity.

Story question: Should the player preserve the old seeds as a library or use them immediately to solve food shortages?
The Quiet Observatory
The Quiet Observatory is a high island containing a star map, a weather instrument, and records of the first sky settlements. It unlocks forecasting and reveals story information about the storm belt.

Story question: The observatory predicts that the storms are not random. Does the town investigate, warn other settlements, or keep the information secret?
The Ruins of Asterfall
Asterfall is a late-game island containing the remains of the first large Skystead. Its buildings are beautiful but unstable. It contains advanced blueprints, historical records, and evidence that previous settlements failed because they over-expanded.

Story question: Should the player restore Asterfall as a monument, salvage it for technology, or leave it untouched?
8. Bridges and travel
Bridges are the most visible symbol of progress. Each connection physically changes the map and creates new routes for residents, resources, and stories.

Early bridges are rope-and-wood structures. Mid-game bridges use reinforced stone, crystal anchors, and wind supports. Late-game routes use airships and suspended transit lines.

Every connection has three values:
- Construction cost.
- Travel capacity.
- Stability during weather events.
A cheap bridge may be useful early but require repairs during storms. A reinforced bridge costs more but keeps trade routes open. The player should be able to see the bridge’s condition and understand the risk before building it.
9. Buildings
Buildings are grouped into four categories.
Survival buildings
- Shelter.
- Water collector.
- Food garden.
- Storage house.
- Clinic.
Renewable infrastructure
- Solar farm.
- Wind turbine.
- Rain reservoir.
- Recycling center.
- Battery or crystal storage.
Community buildings
- Market.
- Community hall.
- School or workshop.
- Garden plaza.
- Festival square.
Exploration and administration
- Beacon tower.
- Weather observatory.
- Airship dock.
- Archive.
- Trade port.
Buildings can be upgraded rather than replaced. An upgraded water collector should visibly gain additional tanks, pipes, and planting beds. An upgraded solar farm should gain more panels and storage equipment. Visual growth should make progress obvious even before the player reads the numbers.
10. Levels, XP, and rewards
The level system represents the player’s growth as a sky steward. XP comes from meaningful achievements:
- Completing missions.
- Connecting islands.
- Discovering landmarks.
- Restoring damaged infrastructure.
- Maintaining sustainability.
- Helping neighboring settlements.
- Surviving major weather events.
A suggested progression is:
Level 1 — Drifter
The player has a single island, a basic shelter site, and simple gathering.

Unlocks: shelter, gathering, first bridge anchor.
Level 2 — Settler
The player can build basic food and water infrastructure.

Unlocks: food garden, water collector, Forest Island route.
Level 3 — Builder
The player can create stronger routes and renewable energy.

Unlocks: solar farm, reinforced bridge, market.
Level 4 — Steward
The player must manage pollution, happiness, and supply chains.

Unlocks: recycling center, community hall, Rain Island upgrades.
Level 5 — Sky Engineer
The player can operate advanced energy and travel systems.

Unlocks: wind turbine, airship dock, weather observatory.
Level 6 — Cloud Mayor
The player leads a connected Skystead and faces the final sustainability challenge.

Unlocks: multi-island town planning, final campaign chapter, Asterfall restoration.

Rewards should change the player’s options. Examples include:
- Efficient Water Collector: produces more water per turn.
- Storm Bracing: reduces bridge damage.
- Seed Library: unlocks more reliable food production.
- Shared Battery: stores surplus renewable energy.
- Airship Logistics: reduces the cost of moving resources.
- Community Charter: reduces happiness loss during rationing.
Cosmetic rewards can include flags, bridge styles, building skins, cloud colors, resident outfits, banners, windmill designs, and town names.
11. Campaign story
The campaign is divided into chapters. Each chapter introduces one new system and advances the history of the sky settlements.
Chapter 1: A Place to Begin
The player arrives at New Haven with a small group of residents. The shelter is damaged, the beacon is dark, and the old bridge has collapsed.

Main objective: Build a shelter and establish a reliable first supply.

Key characters:
- Mara Vale, the practical settlement coordinator.
- Tovin, a bridge builder who trusts rope more than mathematics.
- Pip, a young gardener who collects seeds from every island.
- Cloud, the friendly AI advisor housed in the old beacon.
Chapter ending: The player builds the first bridge and sees the distant sky network light up.
Chapter 2: The First Connection
The player chooses whether to reach Forest Island, Rain Island, or Crystal Island first. Each choice changes the opening strategy and the first group of missions.

Main objective: Connect one resource island and bring its resource system online.

Chapter ending: A message arrives from a distant town that has not spoken to New Haven in years.
Chapter 3: A Town Needs More Than Supplies
New residents arrive. The town gains a market, a small clinic, and the first arguments about growth. Some residents want faster expansion. Others want the town to remain small and stable.

Main objective: Reach a population target without allowing water or happiness to collapse.

Chapter ending: The player discovers that the nearby islands are drifting because of changes in the storm currents.
Chapter 4: The Storm Season
The first major storm season arrives. The player receives warnings through the advisor, but forecasts are imperfect. Bridges, crops, energy systems, and clinics are placed under pressure.

Main objective: Prepare the town and protect essential systems.

Chapter ending: The observatory records an unusual signal from Asterfall.
Chapter 5: The Old Skyway
The player explores the history of the first Skystead. They learn that its leaders expanded quickly, extracted too much crystal energy, and lost control of the island network.

Main objective: Restore the old navigation system without repeating the original settlement’s mistakes.

Chapter ending: The player learns that the storm belt may be a natural system responding to human activity.
Chapter 6: Higher Tomorrows
The final chapter asks the player to connect multiple islands and make the whole network sustainable. There is no single perfect ending. The player’s choices determine which kind of Skystead they create.

Possible endings:
- The Garden Skystead: high food diversity, high happiness, slower expansion.
- The Bright Skystead: high renewable energy and technology, more dependence on crystal infrastructure.
- The Connected Skystead: strong trade and airship routes, more complex maintenance.
- The Quiet Skystead: small population, low consumption, strong ecological stability.
- The Open Skystead: a cooperative network that shares resources with other towns.
12. Quest and mission catalog
Missions should be a mixture of tutorial objectives, resource challenges, exploration goals,
moral choices, and story quests.
Early-game missions
First Steps
Build the first shelter.

Reward: 50 XP, 25 cloud coins, access to basic housing.
A Place for Water
Collect enough water to survive three turns.

Reward: 60 XP, water storage blueprint.
The First Bridge
Connect New Haven to any resource island.

Reward: 100 XP, bridge decoration, access to island production.
A Garden in the Sky
Produce the first food surplus.

Reward: 75 XP, starter garden upgrade.
The Beacon Wakes
Restore the navigation beacon using parts from the home island.

Reward: Cloud advisor access and a revealed map region.
Resource and infrastructure missions
Keep the Lights On
Maintain positive energy for five turns.

Reward: 100 XP, battery storage blueprint.
Water for Everyone
Reach a stable water surplus while housing at least 30 residents.

Reward: 150 XP, efficient water collector.
The Long Span
Build a bridge with a stability rating above 70%.

Reward: reinforced bridge design.
Seeds of Tomorrow
Grow three different crops.

Reward: seed library and increased food diversity.
No Drop Left Behind
Finish a mission with at least 90% of collected water still available.

Reward: water conservation upgrade.
Exploration missions
What the Wind Carries
Reach Mistral Heights and activate one old turbine.

Reward: wind power technology.
The Observatory Door
Find three missing components for the Quiet Observatory.

Reward: weather forecast system.
Letters from Asterfall
Recover a readable message from the ruins.

Reward: story entry, rare blueprint, and 200 XP.
A Place in the Map
Name and document a newly discovered island.

Reward: cosmetic island marker and cloud coins.
Community missions
A Home with a View
Build enough housing without reducing happiness below 60%.

Reward: housing style upgrade.
The First Market Day
Produce a surplus of two resources and open a market.

Reward: trade system.
The Clinic at Dusk
Keep the clinic powered during a weather event.

Reward: medical reserve upgrade.
A Festival Between Storms
Host a festival while maintaining enough food and energy for the next turn.

Reward: festival decoration and happiness bonus.
Everyone Has a Job
Reach a population of 50 while maintaining positive food production.

Reward: workshop building.
Sustainability missions
Higher Tomorrows
Reach 60% sustainability.

Reward: renewable construction discount.
The Clean Route
Connect three islands without using emergency fuel.

Reward: clean transit badge and 250 XP.
Restore, Do Not Replace
Repair a damaged building instead of demolishing it.

Reward: repair workshop.
The Balanced Town
Finish a chapter with water, food, energy, and happiness all above 50%.

Reward: balanced settlement title.
A Sky That Shares
Help a neighboring town without allowing New Haven to enter a shortage.

Reward: cooperative trade routes.
13. Dynamic events
Events make each turn feel different and create moments for the AI advisor to interpret.
Weather events
- Solar storm: temporarily reduces solar output.
- Heavy rain: increases water collection but can damage bridges.
- Heat wave: increases water consumption and reduces crop output.
- Crosswind: increases airship risk and bridge maintenance costs.
- Cloud drought: reduces natural water production.
- Lightning front: threatens exposed infrastructure.
Resource events
- Broken reservoir valve.
- Seed shipment delayed.
- Crystal vein becomes unstable.
- Wind turbine blade cracks.
- Food stores are contaminated.
- A bridge anchor begins to slip.
Social events
- New residents request housing.
- Workers request safer bridge conditions.
- Residents want a public garden.
- A neighboring town asks for help.
- Two communities disagree about water rights.
- A festival competes with an emergency repair project.
Discovery events
- A flock of sky birds reveals a hidden route.
- A dormant beacon begins transmitting.
- An old airship drifts into town.
- A lost child’s map reveals a shortcut.
- A ruined sign points toward Asterfall.
14. The AI advisor
The friendly cloud seen in the concept art is called Cloud. Cloud is the town’s planning assistant, housed in the restored navigation beacon. Cloud does not control the town and does not secretly make decisions for the player. Cloud explains situations, compares options, and points out consequences.

The advisor panel should always use the same structure:
- Current situation.
- Recommendation.
- Reason.
- Trade-off.
- Confidence.
- Optional alternative.
Example:

Recommendation: Connect to Rain Island. Reason: Your water reserve will support only two more turns, and your new residents will increase consumption. Trade-off: The water route delays access to Prism Shelf, which would unlock better energy storage. Confidence: 88%.

Cloud can also summarize a completed turn, explain why happiness changed, suggest a mission strategy, name a discovered island, and provide short story reactions.

The AI must not be responsible for the rules. Resource costs, mission validation, XP, rewards, and win/loss conditions are deterministic game systems. The advisor interprets those systems and communicates them in a friendly way.
15. Interface structure
The main screen is a command center rather than a traditional menu-heavy strategy interface.
Top bar
The top bar shows:
- Cloudbreakers title.
- Energy.
- Water.
- Happiness.
- Player level or turn.
- Current weather condition.
- Profile and settings.
The resource meters should be visible at all times because they represent the player’s immediate priorities.
Center map
The center area displays the connected sky town. Islands should be visually distinct and connected by bridges. Buildings should appear on the islands, and their condition should be visible through small cues such as lights, flags, smoke, water flow, or warning markers.

The map is both a game board and a reward. When a bridge is built, the player should see the town physically become larger.
Left navigation rail
The left rail can contain:
- Build.
- Town overview.
- Sustainability.
- People.
- Map.
- Journal.
- Missions.
The rail should remain simple. It should not hide the main game loop behind multiple layers of menus.
Right advisor panel
The advisor panel is a friendly cloud-shaped card with a clear recommendation. It should never cover the critical state of the game. The player can close it, ask for another explanation, or compare the advice with their own plan.
Mission panel
The mission panel shows active, available, and completed quests. Each mission displays its objective, reward, and progress. Completed missions should produce a short celebration and show exactly what was unlocked.
End-of-turn report
After advancing a turn, show:
- Resources gained.
- Resources consumed.
- Buildings affected.
- Happiness change.
- Sustainability change.
- New event or forecast.
- Mission progress.
This report is important because it helps players learn the cause-and-effect system.
16. Account and progression structure
Players create an account so their town and long-term progression persist between sessions. The account should store:
- Username.
- Player level.
- XP.
- Cloud coins.
- Completed missions.
- Unlocked buildings.
- Upgrade levels.
- Cosmetic items.
- Current town.
- Discovery journal.
The active mission state should be separate from permanent progression. Temporary conditions such as a storm warning, damaged bridge, or current resource balance belong to the current town state. Permanent unlocks belong to the player profile.
17. Failure and recovery
The game should allow failure without making the player feel that the entire account is ruined.

Possible failure states:
- A town enters a shortage.
- A bridge is temporarily closed.
- A building becomes damaged.
- Happiness falls too low.
- A mission expires.
Recovery options include:
- Repairing the building.
- Asking a neighboring island for help.
- Rationing resources.
- Taking a temporary cloud-coin loan.
- Replaying a mission.
- Choosing a different expansion route.
The player should be able to learn from a failure and try again without losing all permanent upgrades.
18. Technical game structure
The game should be divided into five major systems.
Simulation system
The simulation owns turns, resource production, resource consumption, weather effects, building output, population changes, and sustainability calculations.
World system
The world system owns island definitions, island connections, bridge stability, discovery state, routes, and map progression.
Progression system
The progression system owns XP, levels, unlocks, rewards, mission completion, and cosmetic progression.
Narrative system
The narrative system owns chapter progression, dialogue, event text, discovery stories, resident requests, and ending conditions.
Advisor system
The advisor system reads a safe summary of the current game state and returns explanations or recommendations. It should not directly mutate the simulation.

A useful state model is:

Player Profile

- account
- level
- xp
- cloud coins
- permanent unlocks
- cosmetics
- completed story chapters

Town State

- town name
- current turn
- resources
- population
- happiness
- sustainability
- buildings
- islands
- bridges
- active events
- active missions

World State

- discovered islands
- restored landmarks
- neighboring settlements
- chapter flags
- available routes

19. Difficulty and replayability
The campaign should be approachable for first-time players, but provide depth through different expansion routes.

A player who chooses Rain Island first may have strong water stability but weaker early energy. A player who chooses Crystal Island first may unlock technology quickly but risk food and water shortages. A player who prioritizes Forest Island may build rapidly but need to manage ecological pressure.

Replayability can come from:
- Different starting island arrangements.
- Rotating weather patterns.
- Optional challenge cards.
- Different mission order.
- Multiple story choices.
- Town specialization.
- Daily sky challenges.
- Endless mode.
- Cosmetic collection.
The first playable version should not attempt all of these. The campaign and three expansion choices are enough for a strong vertical slice.
20. Recommended first complete version
The smallest version that still feels like the full game should contain:
- Account creation and login.
- One starting island.
- Three resource islands.
- Wood, water, food, energy, happiness, population, and sustainability.
- Shelter, water collector, solar farm, and bridge construction.
- Three story chapters or one complete opening chapter.
- Five missions.
- XP, levels, and three meaningful upgrades.
- Six dynamic events.
- AI Sky Planner recommendations.
- A journal showing discoveries and completed missions.
- A final objective: build a sustainable town with at least three connected islands.
The demo should tell a complete story in approximately five minutes:
1. The player signs in.
2. New Haven begins with almost nothing.
3. The player builds a shelter.
4. The player gathers enough wood for a bridge.
5. Cloud compares Rain Island and Prism Shelf.
6. The player chooses an expansion route.
7. The new island changes the available resources.
8. A mission is completed.
9. The player earns XP and unlocks an upgrade.
10. A weather event tests the decision.
11. The town ends with a visible improvement and a higher sustainability score.
21. The design principle
Every major system should reinforce the same idea:

A town is not sustainable because it has the most buildings. It is sustainable because its choices support people, resources, and the future at the same time.

Cloudbreakers should make expansion feel exciting, but it should also make restraint, repair, cooperation, and preparation feel rewarding. The player is not merely filling a map.

They are deciding what kind of community deserves to exist in the sky.
